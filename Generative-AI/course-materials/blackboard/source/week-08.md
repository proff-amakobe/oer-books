## SECTION 1 — MODULE INTRODUCTION & OBJECTIVES

### 1. Week 8 Introduction & Objectives

#### Module Introduction

Every chapter so far has asked you to make a design decision, which model, which prompt, which retrieval strategy, and defend it in writing. But defend it against what, exactly? So far, mostly against your own reasoning and the concepts covered in each chapter. That's a real skill, but it has a ceiling: this field moves fast enough that a genuinely good decision often depends on knowing what's been tried, what's been shown to work, and what the field currently disagrees about, none of which you can reason your way to from first principles alone.

This chapter is different from the others in one important way: it's not primarily about a new AI capability. It's about a skill this entire book has been assuming you'd eventually need and hasn't yet taught directly, how to find, read, and synthesize the research literature well enough to ground a real technical decision in something more solid than intuition. You're going to need this skill in two places immediately: your Week 8 project proposal, due at the end of this chapter, and every chapter from here forward, which will lean more heavily on primary sources than the earlier, more foundational chapters did.

## SECTION 4 — ASSIGNMENTS

### 4. Week 8 Assignments

The discussion develops a judgment about **What Makes an AI Claim Credible**. The applied work tests that judgment against How a Wrong Benchmark Claim Spreads. The milestone carries the evidence into Literature Review & Project Proposal.

### Assignment 1 — What Makes an AI Claim Credible?

> [SUBMISSION] Type: Discussion Board. Points: 50. Submit one initial post and two peer responses in the Week 8 Discussion item. Use Blackboard’s displayed due dates.

Write an initial post of approximately **350–500 words**. Make a defensible claim, compare alternatives, and use evidence from the assigned material. Connect your judgment to your research-assistant design rather than summarizing the chapter.

#### Discussion Prompt

**Part A — Position:** Choose a specific AI capability claim relevant to your project and identify its original source.

**Part B — Comparison:** Assess the evaluation setup, baseline, source type, and scope of the claimed result. Explain a plausible alternative interpretation.

**Part C — Judgment:** State whether the claim should change your design now, later, or not at all, and identify the missing evidence.

#### Peer Response Guidance

Reply to **two classmates, 150–200 words each**. Challenge an assumption, introduce a counterexample, identify an overlooked trade-off, ask a substantive question, or connect the design to another course concept. Agreement, praise, or summary alone is insufficient. Explain why your contribution matters to the peer’s decision.

### Assignment 2 — Benchmark Claim Investigation + Literature Synthesis

> [SUBMISSION] Type: Applied analysis. Points: 75. Submit one PDF or DOCX to Week 8 Assignment 2. Aim for 600–800 words of analysis plus the requested matrix, diagram, prompts, or evidence appendix; these artifacts are not included in the word guide.

Use **How a Wrong Benchmark Claim Spreads** as the anchor. Your task is to explain and test the design reasoning, not retell the case. Work through the following connected steps with informative headings.

#### Detailed Response Prompt

1. Start from How a Wrong Benchmark Claim Spreads. Select one real, traceable claim; do not accuse a source of contamination without evidence.

2. Create a claim-provenance table covering the original report, one independent discussion or evaluation where available, task, dataset, baseline, metric, and limitations. Mark missing evidence explicitly.

3. Compare what the evidence supports with what a headline might imply. Analyze contamination risk, setup mismatch, or baseline weakness as questions to investigate, not assumed findings.

4. Write a thematic synthesis connecting at least three sources to one project decision. Feed the strongest sources and gaps into your 5–8-source midterm proposal.

#### Evidence and Submission Guidance

Use the same assumptions consistently across the response. A design exercise can be completed as a documented walkthrough; do not disguise a projected outcome as an experiment. Where a task asks for live observations, use accessible tools and retain the relevant outputs. If access prevents the required comparison, request an instructor-provided output set rather than inventing results. Explain what your evidence cannot establish.

> [SUBMISSION] Type: Project portfolio milestone. Points: 50. Submit a PDF or DOCX to Week 8 Project Milestone. Preserve the author’s required elements below and retain an editable copy for Week 16.

#### Detailed Milestone Instructions — Author’s Requirements

This week's milestone is your formal midterm checkpoint. Submit:

1. **5–8 sources** grounding your project's key design decisions so far, drawn from a mix of papers, technical reports, and (where genuinely relevant) high-quality secondary sources.
2. **A synthesized review**, organized by theme per section 8.4, not by chapter or by source.
3. **An honest gaps section**: what's genuinely underexplored in the literature for your specific domain.
4. **A revised project summary**: given what the literature review surfaced, does anything about your Chapter 1 charter or later decisions need to change? Say so explicitly if it does.

#### What Makes a Strong Milestone

A strong **Literature Review & Project Proposal** addresses the actual users and constraints from your charter. Make the decision explicit, explain the rejected alternative, and connect claims to evidence from the week’s reading or applied work. The required elements above control the submission; additional pages or a working implementation do not substitute for missing reasoning.

Use the scope and length stated by the author. Where no length is specified, a focused 1–3 pages plus necessary diagrams or evidence is normally sufficient; the literature review may need more space for its 5–8 sources. Identify one uncertainty or decision to revisit. Label any change to a prior milestone so your portfolio remains coherent.
