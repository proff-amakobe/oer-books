## SECTION 1 — MODULE INTRODUCTION & OBJECTIVES

### 1. Week 1 Introduction & Objectives

#### Module Introduction

Welcome to your journey into the fascinating world of Generative Artificial Intelligence! If you've ever wondered how ChatGPT writes coherent essays, how Midjourney creates stunning artwork from text descriptions, or how GitHub Copilot suggests code completions, you're about to discover the foundational principles that make these remarkable capabilities possible.

This opening chapter lays the groundwork for understanding how machines can create human-like text, images, and other content that often seems indistinguishable from human-generated work. We'll explore the remarkable evolution from simple rule-based systems of the 1960s to today's powerful Large Language Models (LLMs) that can write essays, answer complex questions, engage in creative storytelling, and even help with programming tasks.

But this isn't just a theoretical exploration. Throughout this book, you'll build a real, sustained artifact of your own: the design for an AI-powered research assistant, carried forward and refined chapter by chapter across the entire semester. This chapter isn't where you write code for that assistant, it's where you make the first, most important decision about it: *what problem is it actually solving, and why does that problem call for generative AI rather than something simpler?* Getting that question right now will make every later technical decision easier.

### Assignment 1 — Introduce Yourself + Does This Problem Need Generative AI?

> [SUBMISSION] Type: Discussion Board. Points: 50. Submit one initial post and two peer responses in the Week 1 Discussion item. Use Blackboard’s displayed due dates.

Before the academic response, introduce yourself in **100–150 words**: preferred name, academic/professional background, current AI or generative-AI experience, what you hope to gain, and one possible semester-project domain/problem. Then write the **350–500-word academic response** below. Your total initial post is approximately **450–650 words**.

#### Discussion Prompt

**Part A — The Problem:** Describe a concrete real-world problem, the user who faces it, and the setting in which help is needed.

**Part B — The AI Choice:** Argue whether generation is necessary. Compare the proposed approach with at least one simpler alternative: rules, traditional search/retrieval, or discriminative classification.

**Part C — The Risk:** Identify one concrete limitation or ethical concern from Chapter 1. Explain who bears the risk and how it affects your choice.

#### Peer Response Guidance

Reply to **two classmates, 150–200 words each**. Briefly acknowledge something from the classmate’s introduction before engaging analytically with the argument. Challenge an assumption, introduce a counterexample, identify an overlooked trade-off, ask a substantive question, or connect the design to another course concept. Agreement, praise, or summary alone is insufficient. Explain why your contribution matters to the peer’s decision.

### Assignment 2 — Comparative Model Study + Bounded Automation Analysis

> [SUBMISSION] Type: Applied analysis. Points: 75. Submit one PDF or DOCX to Week 1 Assignment 2. Aim for 600–800 words of analysis plus the requested matrix, diagram, prompts, or evidence appendix; these artifacts are not included in the word guide.

Use **The Associated Press and Automated News Generation** as the anchor. Your task is to explain and test the design reasoning, not retell the case. Work through the following connected steps with informative headings.

#### Detailed Response Prompt

1. Choose 2–3 accessible models. Submit the exact same five prompts to each: a verifiable factual question, a creative-writing task, a multi-step reasoning problem, an ambiguous request, and a recent-information question. Record model/version, access date, and available settings.

2. Build a comparison matrix covering tone, length, factual accuracy, expressed uncertainty, and handling of ambiguity. Verify factual claims against identifiable sources; distinguish a fluent answer from a supported one. Include outputs or precise excerpts as evidence.

3. Read The Associated Press and Automated News Generation case. Explain what makes its task bounded and data-constrained. Compare that setting with the failure you found most consequential in your model study.

4. Recommend a bounded role for generation in your chosen domain. Explain what should remain with a simpler tool or human reviewer. Attach the five prompts and a short record of outputs; do not claim a small comparison proves general superiority.

#### Evidence and Submission Guidance

Use the same assumptions consistently across the response. A design exercise can be completed as a documented walkthrough; do not disguise a projected outcome as an experiment. Where a task asks for live observations, use accessible tools and retain the relevant outputs. If access prevents the required comparison, request an instructor-provided output set rather than inventing results. Explain what your evidence cannot establish.

### Assignment 3 — Research Assistant Milestone 1: Project Charter

> [SUBMISSION] Type: Project portfolio milestone. Points: 50. Submit a PDF or DOCX to Week 1 Project Milestone. Preserve the author’s required elements below and retain an editable copy for Week 16.

#### Detailed Milestone Instructions — Author’s Requirements

This week, produce a one- to two-page **Project Charter** for your semester-long AI-powered research assistant project. It should include:

1. **Domain and problem statement**: What specific problem, in what domain, will your assistant address? Be concrete, not aspirational, "helps small nonprofit staff draft grant reports" is workable; "helps people with research" is not.
2. **Target user**: Who, specifically, will use this? What do they already know, and what do they need help with?
3. **Why generative AI?**: In a substantive paragraph, justify why this problem calls for a generative approach rather than a simpler rule-based system, a search tool, or a traditional classifier. If you can't build this case convincingly, that's valuable information, reconsider the domain before moving on.
4. **What success looks like**: One or two sentences describing what a good outcome would concretely look like for your target user, three months in.

Keep this document. You'll revisit and extend it in every remaining chapter, and it becomes the opening section of your Week 16 final demo.

#### What Makes a Strong Milestone

A strong **Project Charter** addresses the actual users and constraints from your charter. Make the decision explicit, explain the rejected alternative, and connect claims to evidence from the week’s reading or applied work. The required elements above control the submission; additional pages or a working implementation do not substitute for missing reasoning.

Use the scope and length stated by the author. Where no length is specified, a focused 1–3 pages plus necessary diagrams or evidence is normally sufficient; the literature review may need more space for its 5–8 sources. Identify one uncertainty or decision to revisit. Label any change to a prior milestone so your portfolio remains coherent.
