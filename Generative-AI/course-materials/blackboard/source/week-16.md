## SECTION 1 — MODULE INTRODUCTION & OBJECTIVES

### 1. Week 16 Introduction & Objectives

#### Module Introduction

You have carried one research-assistant design through fifteen milestones. Now bring those decisions together and explain what they make possible for your user. Your portfolio should show how model selection, prompts, retrieval, autonomy, evaluation, and responsibility fit the same problem, not fifteen different versions of it.

Chapter 15 calls the Deployment Plan & Forward Look “the final piece before your Week 16 demo.” This week is that handoff. Revisit the questions that opened your charter: what problem are you solving, why does it need generative AI, and what would success look like? Show how the evidence you gathered changed your answers.

Your final demonstration is an opportunity to defend a coherent design, including its limits. A live prototype is optional; a clear storyboarded walkthrough can make the same decisions visible. The aim is to show what you understand, why you chose this approach, and what you would need to learn before putting it in front of real users.

## SECTION 4 — ASSIGNMENTS

### 4. Week 16 Assignments

The discussion develops a judgment about **What Changed in Your Design — and Why**. The applied work tests that judgment against the complete portfolio. The demonstration makes the integrated design and its limits visible.

### Assignment 1 — Final Reflection Discussion: What Changed in Your Design — and Why?

> [SUBMISSION] Type: Discussion Board. Points: 50. Submit one initial post and two peer responses in the Week 16 Discussion item. Use Blackboard’s displayed due dates.

Write an initial post of approximately **350–500 words**. Make a defensible claim, compare alternatives, and use evidence from the assigned material. Connect your judgment to your research-assistant design rather than summarizing the chapter.

#### Discussion Prompt

**Part A — Position:** Identify the most important change between your initial charter and final design. Describe the evidence or feedback that caused it.

**Part B — Comparison:** Compare an early assumption with your current position on capability, evaluation, and responsibility. Explain one trade-off you still cannot resolve.

**Part C — Judgment:** State one next investigation that would improve the design and explain what you would do differently in another project.

#### Peer Response Guidance

Reply to **two classmates, 150–200 words each**. Challenge an assumption, introduce a counterexample, identify an overlooked trade-off, ask a substantive question, or connect the design to another course concept. Agreement, praise, or summary alone is insufficient. Explain why your contribution matters to the peer’s decision.

#### Grading Criteria

| Criterion | Full-credit evidence | Points |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Conceptual application | Applies the week’s concepts accurately to the concrete problem and compares alternatives. | 15 |
| Evidence and judgment | Uses chapter evidence to defend a position, identifies a limitation, and explains a trade-off. | 15 |
| Project connection | Connects the argument to the same research assistant and its users. | 10 |
| Peer engagement | Two substantive replies meet the length guidance and advance the reasoning. | 10 |
| Total |  | 50 |

Cite the chapter or companion selection when it supports your reasoning. Cite outside claims to identifiable sources; an unsupported assertion does not become evidence because a model generated it.

### Assignment 3 — Final Project Demonstration / Presentation

> [SUBMISSION] Type: Design demonstration and defense. Points: 100. Prepare a 10–15 minute presentation with slides or a storyboard and submit those materials through the Week 16 demonstration item. Use the instructor’s designated presentation session or text-based slide-and-notes walkthrough. A recording is not required.

#### Detailed Demonstration Instructions

Explain and defend your system design. A live prototype is optional. A storyboarded walkthrough should show a realistic request, how information and decisions move through the system, the expected response, and a failure or escalation path.

| Segment | What to explain | Suggested time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem and user | Why generative AI; the user and success definition. | 2 min |
| System design | Architecture; model choice; prompt strategy; RAG/knowledge; agent capability if applicable; fine-tuning decision. | 4 min |
| Evidence and responsibility | Evaluation; safety/red-team findings; ethics/fairness; legal/governance issues; deployment plan. | 4 min |
| Synthesis and defense | Most important design change; remaining uncertainty; questions and trade-offs. | 2–5 min |

Use evidence from your portfolio, including evidence that complicated your original plan. Make clear which behavior was observed and which is projected. For a text-based walkthrough, include speaker notes paced for the same 10–15 minute presentation and a short anticipated-questions section. Do not claim production readiness solely because a demo works.

#### What Makes a Strong Final Demonstration

The audience should be able to follow the same problem through every decision. Show how a design limitation affects a user, what would trigger human review, and what further evidence would be needed before deployment. Defend a bounded design instead of promising that every frontier capability belongs in the assistant.

#### Before You Submit

- Check that each required prompt component is visibly addressed, using descriptive headings.
- Match the discussion claim to the analysis evidence and the portfolio decision. Explain a disagreement rather than hiding it.
- Keep references traceable: name the chapter or section, identify external sources, and date time-sensitive evidence.
- Confirm that diagrams and tables are readable and that the submitted files open normally.
- Label assumptions, illustrative examples, measured observations, and projections consistently.
- Read the rubric once as a reviewer. Identify where the evidence for each criterion appears.

#### Portfolio Continuity

The portfolio and demonstration should tell the same story. Resolve any mismatch between slides and the final document, and retain the sources that support important claims. The project ends with a defensible design and a clear account of remaining work; a production deployment is not required.

#### Self-Check for Understanding

Before closing the week, ask yourself: What decision can I now defend more clearly? Which alternative did I reject, and why? What evidence would change my mind? Where does the user experience a failure if my assumptions are wrong? These are reflection prompts to improve the three submissions, not an additional graded assignment.
